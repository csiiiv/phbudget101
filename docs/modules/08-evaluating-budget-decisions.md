## Purpose

Move learners from reading figures to assessing their meaning: applying criteria, using indicators, and judging whether allocations serve needs.

## Learning objectives

After this module, the learner can:

1. Connect needs, policies, allocations, implementation, and outcomes into one evaluation chain.
2. Apply the seven criteria (adequacy, efficiency, effectiveness, equity, sustainability, transparency, accountability) to a budget decision.
3. Use the policy analysis pyramid screen: *Tama ba? Kaya ba? Susuportahan ba?*
4. Read baselines, targets, and performance indicators critically.
5. Identify gaps between plans and budgets, and evaluate distribution across populations and places.
6. Distinguish evidence from assumptions in a budget argument.

## Lessons

### 08.1 From Numbers to Judgment

* Guiding question: Two agencies both spent 90% of their budgets — which one served the public better?
* Concepts: the evaluation chain (needs → policies → allocations → implementation → outcomes); why utilization alone can't answer the question; criteria as the bridge from reading to judging
* Interactive or visual: evaluation chain diagram
* Worked example: none
* Activity and knowledge check: locate weak links in a sample chain; 2-item check

### 08.2 Seven Ways to Judge a Budget

* Guiding question: What makes a budget decision good?
* Concepts: adequacy; efficiency; effectiveness; equity; sustainability; transparency; accountability — each defined with a one-line budget example
* Interactive or visual: criteria wheel/card set
* Worked example: none
* Activity and knowledge check: apply criteria to short scenarios; 3-item check

### 08.3 The Three-Question Screen

* Guiding question: How do you quickly test a proposal before deep analysis?
* Concepts: the policy analysis pyramid — *Tama ba? Kaya ba? Susuportahan ba?* (Is it right? Is it feasible? Will it be supported?): technical correctness/ethics/equity, administrative feasibility, political supportability; how the screen relates to the seven criteria
* Interactive or visual: three-gate visual
* Worked example: the guidebook's four transport proposals worked through the pyramid — protected bike lane networks (passes all three gates with a supportability caveat), car-lane widening (feasible and supportable but fails the first gate), number coding, and elevated expressways; each has a completed three-gate analysis in the source
* Activity and knowledge check: run a sample proposal through the screen; 2-item check

### 08.4 Evidence Versus Assumption

* Guiding question: What separates a strong budget argument from a convincing-sounding one?
* Concepts: baselines and targets; performance indicators and their gaming; plan-vs-budget gap analysis; distributional view (who benefits, where); tracking changes over time; evidence vs assumption
* Interactive or visual: evidence/assumption sorting exercise
* Worked example: none
* Activity and knowledge check: tag statements as evidence or assumption; 3-item check

### 08.5 Weighing Competing Proposals

* Guiding question: Two groups want the same money for different programs — how do you decide?
* Concepts: integrating criteria, screen, and evidence standards into a comparative judgment; writing the reasoning, not just the verdict
* Interactive or visual: int-08 proposal comparison exercise
* Worked example: none
* Activity and knowledge check: the comparison is the activity; check asks for the written rationale's key elements

## Interactive element

* **int-08 Proposal comparison:** side-by-side comparator. Two competing proposals for the same policy problem, with evidence dossiers; learner scores each against the seven criteria and the three-question screen, then writes a short rationale; model answer reveals. Dossiers use the guidebook's protected-bike-lanes vs car-lane-widening pair (competing solutions to the same mobility problem, both fully analyzed in the source; policy content only — no organizations). Fallback: printable comparison worksheet with the same dossiers.

## Assessment

Module check: 6 items across criteria, screen, indicators, and evidence standards.

## Worked examples used

The guidebook's four transport proposals (08.3, int-08): the strongest neutrality-compliant worked examples in the source — complete three-gate analyses of real policy options (bike lanes, car-lane widening, number coding, elevated expressways) with no named advocates. The bike-lanes vs widening pair are competing proposals for the same problem, which is why they anchor int-08.
