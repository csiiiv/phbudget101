## Purpose

Explain the resource side of the budget — where government money comes from and why it is limited — before the course discusses how it is allocated.

## Learning objectives

After this module, the learner can:

1. List the main revenue sources of the national government (taxes, fees and charges, non-tax revenues, borrowing, grants) with a concrete example of each.
2. Distinguish national from local government revenue sources.
3. Explain what a revenue forecast is and why the budget is built on one.
4. Define deficit, surplus, and public debt and explain how they relate.
5. Explain fiscal space and why government cannot fund every proposal.
6. Distinguish budget authority from available cash.

## Lessons

### 02.1 Where the Money Comes From

* Guiding question: When government spends ₱1, where did that peso come from?
* Concepts: tax revenue (income, VAT, excise); fees and charges; dividends and non-tax revenues; borrowing; grants; rough proportions of national revenue mix
* Interactive or visual: revenue composition bar/stacked chart (static, dated figures)
* Worked example: the Sin Tax Reform Law — how an excise-tax change changed the revenue mix and earmarked receipts to health programs (public record: the law, its rates, and enacted allocations; no organization named)
* Activity and knowledge check: sort revenue examples into source categories; 3-item check

### 02.2 National and Local Purses

* Guiding question: Does my city hall spend the same money as the national government?
* Concepts: national vs LGU revenue sources; own-source revenues vs transfers; preview of the National Tax Allotment (depth in Module 6, which also traces the national revenue side from Module 2 into local transfers)
* Interactive or visual: two-column comparison card
* Worked example: none
* Activity and knowledge check: classify revenue items as national or local; 2-item check

### 02.3 Why the Money Runs Out

* Guiding question: If the need is urgent, why can't government just fund it?
* Concepts: revenue forecasts and their uncertainty; deficit and how it is financed; public debt and interest; fiscal space; competing priorities as the zero-sum reality; budget authority vs cash availability
* Interactive or visual: int-10 budget balancer simulation (Phase 3)
* Worked example: none
* Activity and knowledge check: static worksheet activity — given a revenue forecast and three proposals, decide and justify; 3-item check

## Interactive element

* **int-10 Budget balancer:** simulation. Learner allocates a simplified revenue pool across sectors; random events shift revenue/borrowing mid-game; end screen shows service consequences and deficit/debt outcome. Static data, illustrative figures. Fallback: the 02.3 worksheet activity (already designed above) plus static before/after charts.

## Assessment

Module check: 5 items on revenue sources, forecast logic, deficit/debt, and fiscal space.

## Worked examples used

Sin Tax Reform Law (02.1): a public-fact worked example of revenue-side policy — the law, its rate structure, and its earmarked health allocations are all verifiable public record, which makes it the strongest neutrality-compliant example of "tax policy changes what government can fund." Author from the law itself and DOF/DBM published data, not from any advocacy account.
